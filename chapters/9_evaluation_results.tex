\chapter{Evaluation and Results}
\label{ch:evaluation_results}

Evaluating a language-agnostic static analysis framework presents
significant empirical challenges. First, obtaining an absolute,
manually verified ground truth for large-scale real-world codebases
is practically infeasible. Second, comparative evaluation against
existing analyzers is complicated by substantial behavioral and
granularity disparities: different tools operate at varying levels of
abstraction (from fine-grained function calls and variable references
to coarse-grained class or translation-unit components), employ
divergent relational taxonomies, and encode things differently.
To address these challenges, our evaluation is divided into three parts:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ground-Truth Validation}: Verifying extraction
    accuracy against synthetic micro-benchmarks with known oracles,
    covering both basic language constructs and language-specific
    idioms in isolation.
  \item \textbf{Cross-Tool Structural Comparison}: Comparing OmniDeps
    against established analyzers on real-world repositories, using a
    graph harmonization framework that aims to reconcile differences in
    granularity, metamodels, and edge taxonomies.
  \item \textbf{Runtime Performance Benchmarking}: Measuring
    execution time, analysis throughput, and scalability across
    projects of different sizes under a controlled environment.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this chapter is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:research_questions} introduces the research
questions. Sections~\ref{sec:ground_truth_methodology},
\ref{sec:cross_tool_comparison}, and
\ref{sec:performance_benchmarking} describe the methodology for each
of the three evaluation parts, and
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_results} presents the experimental results.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

The empirical and theoretical evaluation is guided by four central
Research Questions (RQs):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{(Language Construct Coverage)}: \\
    \textit{Does OmniDeps adequately cover the structural constructs and
    dependencies of all supported programming languages?}

  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{(Cross-Tool Structural Alignment)}: \\
    \textit{How closely do the dependency graphs extracted by
      OmniDeps align with the representations produced by established
      single-language and multi-language architectural analyzers on
    large-scale open-source software systems?}

  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{(Typological Concordance and
    Discrepancy Analysis)}: \\
    \textit{In which specific categories of software dependencies
      (structural containment, inheritance hierarchies, behavioral
      invocations, module inclusion) do cross-tool discrepancies
      concentrate, and what semantic assumptions or architectural
    trade-offs account for observed divergences?}

  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] \textbf{(Computational Scalability and Throughput)}: \\
    \textit{How do the execution runtime and analysis throughput of
      OmniDeps compare against state-of-the-art baselines when
      analyzing repositories of varying scale, from compact libraries
    to multi-million-line codebases?}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Motivation and Rationale.}
These research questions systematically evaluate whether a
lightweight, syntax-query static analyzer can serve as an effective
alternative to traditional compiler-coupled tools:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1} verifies the baseline soundness of our
    extraction heuristics in a controlled setting before scaling to
    complex repositories. Answering this question establishes
    whether Concrete Syntax Tree queries are sufficiently
    expressive to capture core language constructs across diverse
    programming paradigms.
  \item \textbf{RQ2} evaluates real-world applicability, as
    synthetic benchmarks cannot capture the architectural
    complexity of realistic software. Answering this question
    demonstrates whether the language-agnostic extraction strategy
    generalizes to large repositories and yields dependency graphs
    comparable to mature, compiler-based analyzers.
  \item \textbf{RQ3} examines the nature of discrepancies, as
    global similarity metrics hide where and why tools disagree. By
    isolating divergences across containment, inheritance, call
    dependencies, and imports, this question reveals where syntax
    queries match compiler precision and where compiler-level type
    checking remains necessary.
  \item \textbf{RQ4} assesses operational viability for continuous
    integration pipelines, where analysis must scale predictably.
    Answering this question determines whether bypassing heavy
    compiler frontends and build-system configurations delivers
    measurable runtime speedups and linear scalability across
    codebases of varying size.
\end{itemize}
Together, answering these questions allows us to evaluate the
central hypothesis of this thesis: that syntax-directed queries
combined with decoupled name resolution can deliver reliable
architectural dependency graphs with high throughput and minimal
operational overhead.

\section{Ground-Truth Validation}
\label{sec:ground_truth_methodology}

During the development of a static analysis framework, verifying that
individual language constructs and dependencies are faithfully
captured requires a controlled testing environment. In this
validation track, the dependency graph automatically extracted by the
system is programmatically compared against a manually defined ground
truth for each benchmark. To evaluate construct coverage across
paradigms, a dedicated benchmark suite of synthetic source files was
created for each supported language (C, C++, Java, Rust, and Python).
Rather than complete software applications, these suites consist of
minimal source files specifically crafted to exercise and isolate
key architectural constructs, including module definitions,
structured types, function declarations, inheritance hierarchies, and
method invocations.

\subsection{The Oracle}
\label{subsec:micro_benchmarks}

For each language benchmark, the ground truth is formalized in a YAML
manifest file that acts as the architectural oracle against which the
extracted graph is verified. The manifest explicitly defines:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Target Files}: The list of  source files that
    compose the benchmark suite, ensuring the analyzer parses only the
    intended code.
  \item \textbf{Expected Nodes}: The architectural entities that the
    system must recognize. Each entry specifies the entity's Fully
    Qualified Name and its structural category (such as modules,
    classes, structs, traits, enums, or functions).
  \item \textbf{Expected Edges}: The semantic dependencies connecting
    the entities. Each relationship specifies a unique test
    identifier, the source and sink entity names, and the expected
    relationship kind (such as function calls, type inheritance, type
    usages, field accesses, or module imports).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Graph Verification and Matching Logic}
\label{subsec:graph_verification_logic}

To automate the evaluation, a dedicated test harness within OmniDeps
executes the complete extraction pipeline on the benchmark source
files and programmatically verifies adherence to the YAML manifest.

Because the analyzer generates a deeply nested, strongly typed
Intermediate Representation while the manifest relies on flat string
identifiers, the validation logic operates through three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Graph Flattening and Indexing}: The runner flattens
    the extracted component hierarchy into dot-separated fully
    qualified names. It constructs two lookup indices: a node index
    mapping each canonical identifier to its extracted component
    kind, and an adjacency index mapping each source identifier to
    its set of target sinks.
  \item \textbf{Node Verification}: The algorithm iterates through
    the expected nodes in the manifest and checks whether their
    identifier exists in the extracted node index, recording whether
    the entity was discovered and whether its extracted kind matches
    the specification.
  \item \textbf{Edge Verification}: For each expected edge, the
    runner performs a two-step validation:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textit{Endpoint Verification}: It ensures that both the
        source and sink entities were successfully extracted. If
        either endpoint is missing, the edge immediately fails
        validation due to a missing endpoint, isolating whether the
        defect stems from entity extraction or relational resolution.
      \item \textit{Adjacency Verification}: If both endpoints exist,
        the algorithm queries the adjacency index to verify whether a
        directed dependency linking the source to the sink was established.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metrics and Reporting}
\label{subsec:accuracy_metrics}

The benchmark measures extraction accuracy for both nodes and edges
as the fraction of expected elements successfully recovered:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Acc}_{\text{nodes}} = \frac{|\{v \in V_{\text{expected}} \mid
  v \in V_{\text{extracted}}\}|}{|V_{\text{expected}}|}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{Acc}_{\text{edges}} = \frac{|\{e \in E_{\text{expected}} \mid
  e \in E_{\text{extracted}}\}|}{|E_{\text{expected}}|}
\end{equation}

In addition to summary statistics, the test harness generates
detailed reports in Markdown and JSON formats. For each expected
node, the report records whether it was extracted and whether its
component kind matches the specification. For each expected edge, it
details the existence of both endpoints, the presence of the
connecting edge, and the correctness of the relationship kind. This
detailed feedback facilitates debugging during development and
ensures continuous regression tracking across commits.

\section{Cross-Tool Structural Comparison}
\label{sec:cross_tool_comparison}

While synthetic micro-benchmarks validate language features in
isolation, answering \textbf{RQ2} and \textbf{RQ3} requires evaluating
real-world software systems. However, directly comparing dependency
graphs produced by independent static analysis tools is difficult.
Analyzers operate under different metamodels, export formats, and
levels of granularity. Without a dedicated alignment process,
directly intersecting these graphs results in artificially low
agreement caused by representational differences rather than actual
semantic disagreements.

This section presents the harmonization framework used to map
heterogeneous dependency graphs into a common canonical
representation, defines the comparative metrics, and introduces the
evaluation dataset and baseline tools.

\subsection{Graph Harmonization Framework}
\label{subsec:graph_harmonization}
\label{sec:graph_harmonization_framework}

\subsubsection{The Metamodel Heterogeneity Problem}
\label{subsec:metamodel_heterogeneity}

State-of-the-art analyzers conceptualize software structure at
differing levels of abstraction:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Granularity Divergence}: Fine-grained tools (such as
      \textit{Depends} \cite{depends_github} and \textit{Entangle}
    \cite{teruzzi_2024}) extract AST-level variables, functions, and
    method invocations as distinct graph vertices. Conversely,
    architectural analyzers (such as \textit{Arcan}
    \cite{fontana_arcan_2017}) focus primarily on coarse-grained
    structural components (specifically packages, classes, and
      interfaces in object-oriented environments (Java), and physical
    source/header compilation units in procedural codebases (C/C++)).
  \item \textbf{Implicit vs. Explicit Structural Hierarchy}: Certain
    graph models represent modular containment through explicit
    directed edges (e.g., $\texttt{belongsTo}$ or
    $\texttt{ModuleContainment}$), whereas compound graph
    specifications (such as Cytoscape.js
    \cite{franz_cytoscapejs_2016}) represent hierarchy geometrically
    via node nesting attributes (\texttt{parent} pointers), omitting
    explicit containment edge primitives.
  \item \textbf{Relational Taxonomy Mismatch}: Each tool employs
    proprietary relationship vocabularies. For instance, a method
    invocation may be classified as \texttt{Calls} in one tool,
    \texttt{call} in another, or subsumed into a generic
    \texttt{dependsOn} relation in an architectural smell detector.
    Similarly, subtype relationships may be split into
    \texttt{extend} and \texttt{implement}, or unified as
    \texttt{isChildOf} or \texttt{IsA}.
  \item \textbf{Namespace and Identifier Disparities}: Differing path
    separators (\texttt{::} in C++/Rust, \texttt{/} in file paths,
    \texttt{.} in Java/Python), varying handling of nested anonymous
    scopes, and synthetic root prefixes prevent exact string matching
    of entity names.
\end{itemize}

To overcome these structural disparities, we define a graph
normalization pipeline that transforms any tool's raw extracted graph
$G_{\text{raw}} = (V_{\text{raw}}, E_{\text{raw}})$ into a
standardized architectural dependency graph $G_{\text{arch}} =
(V_{\text{arch}}, E_{\text{arch}})$.

\subsubsection{Entity Canonicalization and Classification}
\label{subsec:entity_canonicalization}

Because static analyzers emit identifiers in tool- and
language-specific formats (e.g., using double colons in C++ or
slashes in file paths), the harmonization pipeline first normalizes
raw identifier strings into canonical dot-separated qualified paths
(implemented via \texttt{normalize\_identifier} in the
\texttt{graph-synchron} adapters).

Extracted entities are then classified by architectural granularity.
We partition entities into two disjoint groups:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Architectural Entities} ($V_{\text{arch}}$):
    Coarse-grained structural containers that define module and type
    boundaries, including packages, modules, classes, interfaces,
    traits, and enums. These represent the primary units of
    architectural organization (\texttt{is\_type\_not\_volatile} in
    the adapters).
  \item \textbf{Fine-Grained Entities} ($V_{\text{vol}}$): AST-level
    sub-components residing within architectural containers, such as
    methods, functions, fields, and variables.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Semantic Entity Lifting}
\label{subsec:semantic_lifting}

To enable meaningful comparisons between fine-grained analyzers
(which report method calls and field accesses) and coarse-grained
analyzers (which report dependencies between classes or packages),
member-level entities must be projected onto their enclosing
architectural containers.

For each fine-grained entity, the adapter navigates up the syntactic
containment hierarchy using \texttt{get\_parent} until it reaches the
nearest enclosing architectural entity. This projection $\lambda(v)$
maps any vertex to its architectural container:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda(v) =
  \begin{cases}
    v & \text{if } v \text{ is an architectural entity} \\
    \lambda(\text{parent}(v)) & \text{if } v \text{ is a fine-grained member}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

When applied to edges, any raw dependency $\langle u, v \rangle$
between fine-grained members is lifted to an architectural dependency
between their respective containers $\langle \lambda(u), \lambda(v) \rangle$.

Crucially, when both endpoints belong to the same architectural
entity ($\lambda(u) = \lambda(v)$), the interaction represents an
internal implementation detail, such as a private helper method
invocation or an access to an internal field. These intra-component
self-loops are suppressed, ensuring that comparative evaluation
focuses exclusively on couplings between distinct architectural components.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Containment Synthesis}
\label{subsec:structural_closure}

Architectural analysis relies heavily on hierarchical namespace
structures to evaluate modular boundaries and detect package-level
design flaws. However, static analysis tools represent containment in
fundamentally different ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Compound graph formats (such as Cytoscape JSON) encode
    containment implicitly through node nesting attributes (a
    \texttt{parent} pointer) rather than explicit dependency edges.
  \item Other tools emit only leaf classes, leaving intermediate
    namespace packages implicit.
\end{itemize}

To ensure structural consistency across formats,
\texttt{graph-synchron} applies two harmonization steps during loading:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Intermediate Package Synthesis}: For every entity
    with a qualified path, the adapter automatically generates any
    missing intermediate package nodes along the hierarchy.
  \item \textbf{Explicit Containment Closure}: For every entity with
    a valid parent, an explicit directed containment edge
    (\texttt{belongsTo}) is created connecting the child to its
    enclosing container.
\end{enumerate}
This synthesis guarantees that compound graphs and edge-based graphs
can be compared under identical structural assumptions.

\subsubsection{Unified Relational Taxonomy}
\label{subsec:edge_taxonomy_unification}

Existing static analysis tools use disparate vocabularies to describe
dependency relationships. For instance, a method invocation may be
classified as \texttt{Calls} in one tool, \texttt{call} in another,
or subsumed into a generic \texttt{dependsOn} relation in an
architectural smell detector. Similarly, subtype relationships may be
distinguished into \texttt{extend} and \texttt{implement}, or unified
under a general \texttt{isChildOf} relation.

To reconcile these differences, \texttt{graph-synchron} defines a
translation function (\texttt{translate\_label}) that maps
tool-specific edge labels into a common taxonomy of five
architectural relation classes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{belongsTo}: Physical or logical containment,
    including classes nested in packages or inner classes within outer types.
  \item \texttt{isChildOf}: Generalization and subtyping
    relationships between structured types of the same paradigm.
  \item \texttt{isImplementationOf}: Contract implementations, such
    as interface realizations or Rust trait implementation blocks.
  \item \texttt{includes}: File inclusion directives
    (\texttt{\#include}) and explicit module imports.
  \item \texttt{dependsOn}: Behavioral, usage, and data-flow
    dependencies, aggregating method calls, field references,
    parameter types, return types, instantiations, and exception throws.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparative Graph Metrics}
\label{subsec:comparative_metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

With all graphs mapped to canonical, lifted representations $G = (V,
E)$, structural concordance and divergence are quantified through
set-based and statistical indices.

\subsubsection{Graph Similarity via Smoothed Jaccard Index}
\label{subsec:jaccard_similarity}

The primary measure of cross-tool structural agreement between two
normalized graphs $G_A = (V_A, E_A)$ and $G_B = (V_B, E_B)$ is the
\textbf{Jaccard Similarity Index} \cite{ortin_empirical_2022}. To
ensure numerical stability when evaluating sparse graphs, submodules
with empty edge sets, or boundary components, we employ
\textbf{Laplace-smoothed} Jaccard formulations:

\begin{definition}[Smoothed Node Jaccard Similarity]
  The structural agreement between the extracted vertex sets $V_A$
  and $V_B$ is defined as:
  \begin{equation}
    J_{\text{nodes}}(G_A, G_B) = \frac{|V_A \cap V_B| + 1}{|V_A \cup V_B| + 1}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Smoothed Edge Jaccard Similarity]
  Let each edge $e \in E$ be uniquely indexed by the tuple $\langle
  \text{source}, \text{target} \rangle$ (for unlabelled structural
  comparison) or $\langle \text{source}, \text{target}, \text{label}
  \rangle$ (for typed relational comparison). The edge agreement is
  defined as:
  \begin{equation}
    J_{\text{edges}}(G_A, G_B) = \frac{|E_A \cap E_B| + 1}{|E_A \cup E_B| + 1}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent
\textbf{Properties of the Metric}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $J(G_A, G_B) \in (0, 1]$ for all non-empty graphs, reaching
    $1.0$ if and only if $V_A = V_B$ (or $E_A = E_B$).
  \item In the presence of empty edge sets ($|E_A \cup E_B| = 0$),
    the smoothed index evaluates to $1.0$, correctly reflecting the
    agreement that no dependencies exist, rather than producing an
    undefined $\frac{0}{0}$ indeterminate form.
  \item For realistic codebases where $|V| \gg 1$ and $|E| \gg 1$,
    the additive Laplace smoothing factor ($+1$) introduces
    negligible variance while asymptotically converging to standard
    Jaccard similarity.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Directional Discrepancy Indices}
\label{subsec:discrepancy_indices}

While the Jaccard index summarizes global bidirectional overlap, it
is inherently symmetric: it cannot diagnose \textit{which} tool
extracted a richer architectural graph or identify the nature of
missing dependencies. To measure directional asymmetry, we define two
complementary discrepancy metrics normalized by the shared agreement space:

\begin{definition}[Superiority Index (Relative Novelty)]
  The degree to which analyzer $A$ discovers entities or dependencies
  that are absent in analyzer $B$, relative to their shared
  intersection, is given by:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{Sup}(A, B) =
    \begin{cases}
      \frac{|A \setminus B|}{|A \cap B|} & \text{if } |A \cap B| > 0 \\
      1.0 & \text{if } |A \cap B| = 0 \text{ and } |A \setminus B| > 0 \\
      0.0 & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Inferiority Index (Relative Omission)]
  Conversely, the degree to which analyzer $A$ fails to discover
  entities or dependencies that are successfully extracted by analyzer $B$ is:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{Inf}(A, B) = \text{Sup}(B, A) =
    \begin{cases}
      \frac{|B \setminus A|}{|A \cap B|} & \text{if } |A \cap B| > 0 \\
      1.0 & \text{if } |A \cap B| = 0 \text{ and } |B \setminus A| > 0 \\
      0.0 & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent
These indices provide intuitive diagnostic semantics:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\text{Sup}(A, B) = 0$ indicates that all elements extracted
    by $A$ are confirmed by $B$ ($A \subseteq B$).
  \item $\text{Sup}(A, B) = 0.5$ implies that for every two shared
    dependencies agreed upon by both tools, tool $A$ identifies one
    additional dependency not seen by $B$.
  \item Comparing $\text{Sup}(\text{OmniDeps}, \text{Baseline})$
    against $\text{Inf}(\text{OmniDeps}, \text{Baseline})$
    immediately reveals whether OmniDeps behaves conservatively (high
    precision, lower recall) or aggressively (extracting deeper
    dependency chains at the risk of over-approximation).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Per-Type Edge Discrepancy}
\label{subsec:categorical_discordance}

To understand where structural agreements and divergences
concentrate, discrepancies are analyzed per relationship type
(implemented via \texttt{pairwise\_edge\_diff} in
\texttt{graph-synchron}). For each relationship category $\kappa$
(such as \texttt{belongsTo}, \texttt{isChildOf}, \texttt{dependsOn},
and \texttt{includes}), we compute the directional discrepancy rates:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{A \setminus B}(\kappa) = \frac{|E_A^{(\kappa)} \setminus
  E_B^{(\kappa)}|}{|E_A^{(\kappa)}|}, \qquad
  \Delta_{B \setminus A}(\kappa) = \frac{|E_B^{(\kappa)} \setminus
  E_A^{(\kappa)}|}{|E_B^{(\kappa)}|}
\end{equation}
where $E_A^{(\kappa)}$ denotes the set of edges of type $\kappa$
extracted by tool $A$. This per-category breakdown isolates whether
observed divergences stem from containment hierarchies, inheritance
modeling, or behavioral call dependencies.

\subsection{Evaluation Dataset and Baseline Analyzers}
\label{subsec:dataset_and_baselines}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Dataset}
\label{subsec:dataset_selection}

To evaluate structural alignment and performance, an empirical
dataset of 156 open-source software projects was collected across the
five supported programming languages (C, C++, Java, Python, and
Rust). In total, the dataset accounts for nearly 98 million lines of
code and over 550,000 source files (measured via CLOC).

The repositories were gathered from GitHub to cover a broad spectrum
of software sizes and domains. They range from small utilities and
libraries (such as \texttt{fd} or \texttt{requests}) to large-scale
distributed systems, runtime environments, and engines (such as
\texttt{elasticsearch}, \texttt{godot}, or \texttt{redis}). Selecting
widely adopted open-source projects ensures that the static analyzers
are evaluated against realistic codebases, featuring varied
architectural patterns, module layouts, and idiomatic language constructs.

\subsubsection{Baseline Analyzers}
\label{subsec:baseline_analyzers}

OmniDeps is benchmarked against three static analysis tools, chosen
to contrast different parsing and extraction strategies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Arcan} \cite{fontana_arcan_2017}: An architectural
    analysis framework and architectural smell detector. Arcan relies
    on compiler-based front-ends (Spoon for Java, Clang AST for
    C/C++), providing semantic type resolution at the cost of strong
    language coupling and high runtime overhead.
  \item \textbf{Depends} \cite{depends_github}: A multi-language
    dependency analyzer that employs ANTLR-generated grammar parsers
    for C/C++, Java, and Python, emitting Dependency Structure
    Matrices (DSM) and fine-grained call and usage dependencies.
  \item \textbf{Entangle} \cite{teruzzi_2024}: A direct predecessor
    research prototype developed within our laboratory. Entangle
    abandoned compiler front-ends in favor of Tree-sitter AST parsing
    and dynamic scope climbing for C and Java, serving as an

\section{Runtime Performance Benchmarking}
\label{sec:performance_benchmarking}
\label{sec:benchmarking_protocol}

To answer \textbf{RQ4}, an experimental protocol was defined to
measure execution time, scalability, and computational throughput
across repositories of varying scale.

\subsection{Execution Environment and Measurement Protocol}
\label{subsec:execution_sampling}

To account for operating system scheduling noise, cache warmup
effects, and runtime variations, the benchmarking framework enforces
the following execution protocols:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-Iteration Sampling}: Every tool is executed
    over $k$ independent iterations ($k \ge 10$ for runtime
    benchmarking, $k = 1$ for structural deliverable extraction). For
    each project and tool, the sample mean execution time $\mu$ and
    standard deviation $\sigma$ are calculated:
    \begin{equation}
      \mu = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k t_i, \qquad \sigma =
      \sqrt{\frac{1}{k - 1} \sum_{i=1}^k (t_i - \mu)^2}
    \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Hardware and OS Isolation}: On supported platforms,
    the benchmarking harness pins execution to a dedicated physical
    CPU core (e.g., via affinity masks) to eliminate context
    switching, thread migration, and scheduler jitter.
  \item \textbf{Configurable Execution Timeouts}: When analyzing
    large codebases, complex macro expansions or cyclic grammar
    constructs can induce pathological backtracking loops in baseline
    tools. A configurable timeout $T_{\text{max}}$ (e.g., 30 minutes)
    is enforced. Runs exceeding $T_{\text{max}}$ are recorded as
    timeouts without stalling batch benchmark execution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Throughput and Scalability Metrics}
\label{subsec:scalability_metrics}

Scalability is evaluated across two primary dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Analysis Throughput} ($\Theta$): The processing speed
    measured in source lines of code analyzed per unit of wall-clock time:
    \begin{equation}
      \Theta = \frac{\text{CLOC}}{\mu_t} \quad
      \left[\frac{\text{lines}}{\text{second}}\right]
    \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Relative Speedup} ($S$): The execution speedup
    achieved by OmniDeps relative to comparator baseline $B$:
    \begin{equation}
      S_{B} = \frac{\mu_{t, B}}{\mu_{t, \text{OmniDeps}}}
    \end{equation}
    where $S_B > 1.0$ indicates superior performance by OmniDeps.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Structural Agreement in Java.}
In Java, OmniDeps exhibits consistent structural alignment across all
three comparator tools. When compared against Depends ($N = 31$),
OmniDeps attains a mean $J_{\text{nodes}}$ of 0.814 (median 0.849) and a
mean $J_{\text{edges}}$ of 0.587 (median 0.609). The entity omission rate
remains low ($\text{Inf}_{\text{nodes}} = 0.021$, median 0.004), showing

Finally, against Entangle ($N = 19$), node and edge similarities reach
$0.770$ (median 0.782) and $0.600$ (median 0.598), respectively. Edge
similarity converges to approximately $0.58$--$0.60$ across the three
baselines, showing that package resolution and entity lifting produce
comparable dependency graphs despite differences in the underlying parsers.

\paragraph{Entity Alignment and Call Resolution in Python.}
On Python repositories ($N = 29$), OmniDeps and Depends attain an
average node Jaccard similarity of 0.852 (median 0.882), with an entity
omission rate of just $\text{Inf}_{\text{nodes}} = 0.048$. Several projects
exceed 0.90 in node agreement, including \texttt{uvicorn} (0.973),
\texttt{networkx} (0.970), \texttt{requests} (0.958), \texttt{fastapi} (0.952),
\texttt{celery} (0.949), and \texttt{sqlalchemy} (0.946). This high concordance
reflects the harmonization of method-local test fixtures, which in dynamic testing
frameworks declare throwaway mock classes within test methods. When evaluating all
extracted dependency edges, edge agreement averages 0.323 (median 0.300); when
isolating purely behavioral relations by excluding unmodeled syntactic import
statements, edge agreement reaches an average of 0.368 (median 0.387), with median
edge superiority dropping below parity to 0.889. As analyzed in Section
\ref{subsec:results_rq3}, the remaining divergence reflects the inherent boundaries
of static analysis in dynamically typed languages without pervasive type hints.

\paragraph{Deconstructing Edge Superiority in Python: Genuine Dependencies vs. Import Asymmetry.}
A noticeable pattern in the Python evaluation is the elevated edge superiority
index ($\text{Sup}_{\text{edges}} = 1.473$ mean, reaching $3.498$ on \texttt{sympy},
$3.146$ on \texttt{scikit-learn}, and $2.461$ on \texttt{pydantic}), indicating that
OmniDeps captures substantially more dependency edges than Depends. A systematic
structural diff between the extracted graphs clarifies that these additional edges
are not extraction hallucinations, but represent genuine dependencies stemming from
two distinct sources:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Categorical Import Asymmetry}: The largest component of unique edges
    in OmniDeps ($A \setminus B$) is syntactic module inclusion. OmniDeps extracts every
    explicit syntactic \texttt{import} and \texttt{from ... import ...} directive as a
    typed \texttt{includes} edge, capturing thousands of dependencies on large codebases
    (18,494 in \texttt{sympy}, 8,901 in \texttt{django}, and 5,982 in \texttt{scikit-learn}).
    In contrast, Depends Python omits module-level import statements entirely, emitting only
    a negligible number of coarse package-level relations (only 47 in \texttt{sympy} and
    22 in \texttt{scikit-learn}). Because the unweighted Jaccard denominator pools all
    relation types, this asymmetry alone constitutes $50.6\%$ of all unique edges in
    \texttt{sympy} and $59.4\%$ in \texttt{scikit-learn}.
  \item \textbf{High-Fidelity Inheritance Resolution}: The remaining excess edges
    represent legitimate behavioral and subtyping relationships where OmniDeps exhibits
    higher recall than Depends. In \texttt{scikit-learn}, OmniDeps identifies 1,041
    subtyping edges (\texttt{isChildOf}) compared to only 491 identified by Depends
    (+636 genuine edges). These encompass foundational architectural patterns such as
    estimator mixins:
    \texttt{QuantileTransformer $\to$ BaseEstimator},
    \texttt{QuantileRegressor $\to$ BaseEstimator},
    \texttt{LatentDirichletAllocation $\to$ BaseEstimator},
    \texttt{DictVectorizer $\to$ TransformerMixin},
    \texttt{GradientBoostingRegressor $\to$ RegressorMixin}, and
    \texttt{ColumnTransformer $\to$ \_BaseComposition}.
    Depends misses these relationships because relative imports (\texttt{from ..base import BaseEstimator})
    without static type annotations are frequently classified as speculative
    \texttt{CandidateTypes} and discarded. OmniDeps resolves relative modules deterministically
    via Scope Tree traversal. Similarly, in \texttt{sympy}, OmniDeps captures 604 additional
    inheritance edges linking mathematical function hierarchies (\texttt{TrigonometricFunction},
    \texttt{HyperbolicFunction}, \texttt{catalan}, \texttt{bernoulli}, \texttt{LeviCivita},
    \texttt{erfinv}, \texttt{expint}) back to their root class \texttt{DefinedFunction}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The Polyglot Workspace Traversal Effect across the Benchmark.}
A broader architectural factor contributing to elevated superiority across the
entire 156-project evaluation is OmniDeps' language-agnostic workspace traversal.
Traditional static analysis tools operate as single-language silos governed by
build-system configurations: Depends Python parses only \texttt{.py} files, Arcan parses
only \texttt{.java} files within Maven/Gradle targets, and Entangle parses only Cargo crates.
In contrast, OmniDeps recursively traverses the entire repository workspace, parsing
all source files that match any supported grammar (C, C++, Java, Python, Rust) without
relying on build-system manifests.

Real-world software repositories are frequently polyglot. Modern Python packages
regularly incorporate native extensions for performance: in \texttt{pydantic}, OmniDeps
recursively parses the compiled Rust core (\texttt{pydantic-core/src/}), extracting native
structs, validators, and serializers that the Python-only analyzer in Depends completely
ignores. Similarly, C and C++ repositories frequently contain auxiliary Python build
scripts, code generators, and test harnesses (as in \texttt{redis}, \texttt{git}, and
\texttt{FFmpeg}), while Java projects contain native JNI C/C++ bindings. Because OmniDeps
faithfully extracts entities and dependencies across all languages present in the repository,
whereas single-language baselines restrict extraction strictly to primary source trees,
the multi-language components appear exclusively in OmniDeps' graph ($A \setminus B$).
This structural scope difference naturally inflates the superiority metric ($\text{Sup}$)
across all 156 projects in the dataset, reflecting OmniDeps' holistic polyglot coverage
rather than false-positive over-extraction.

\paragraph{Impact of Translation-Unit Preprocessing in C and C++.}
In C and C++, analyzing raw source code without macro expansion and
header resolution makes it difficult to link types across files.
As reported in Table \ref{tab:results_depends}, preprocessing translation
units (\texttt{clang -E}) substantially improves concordance compared to raw
source analysis in both languages. In C, node superiority
($\text{Sup}_{\text{nodes}}$) decreases from 14.017 to 7.218, while edge
Jaccard similarity increases from 0.115 to 0.136. On small,
self-contained libraries such as \texttt{antirez/sds}, both tools extract
identical graphs ($J_{\text{nodes}} = J_{\text{edges}} = 1.0$). On larger
systems, preprocessing provides moderate agreement: \texttt{sqlite}
reaches $J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.399$ and $J_{\text{edges}} = 0.340$,
\texttt{redis} achieves $0.311$ and $0.157$, and \texttt{FFmpeg} attains
$0.308$ and $0.326$.

In C++, preprocessing increases average edge similarity from 0.081 to
0.165, while reducing edge superiority from 58.836 to 9.962. Notable
agreement is observed on projects with clean modular boundaries, such as
\texttt{ninja} ($J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.491$, $J_{\text{edges}} = 0.410$),
\texttt{duckdb} ($J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.358$, $J_{\text{edges}} = 0.415$),
\texttt{aria2} ($J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.402$, $J_{\text{edges}} = 0.387$), and
\texttt{google/re2} ($J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.360$, $J_{\text{edges}} = 0.459$).

\subsubsection{File-Level vs. Type-Level Analysis: Arcan vs. OmniDeps
in C and C++}
\label{subsubsec:arcan_c_cpp_divergence}

Comparing OmniDeps and Arcan directly on C and C++ repositories results in
near-zero Jaccard similarity ($J_{\text{nodes}} \approx 0.0$,
$J_{\text{edges}} \approx 0.0$). This outcome is not due to extraction
errors, but to different architectural modeling choices:

In Java, both tools analyze code at the class and package level. Because
both tools represent classes and interfaces as nodes, their graphs align
well (Table \ref{tab:results_arcan}).

In C and C++, however, the tools work at different levels of granularity.
Arcan focuses on detecting architectural smells (such as circular
dependencies between files) and represents code at the file level: its nodes
are implementation files (\texttt{IMPL\_FILE}) and header files
(\texttt{HEADER}), while dependencies represent file-to-file calls or
inclusions. Arcan does not extract user-defined types (structs, unions, or
classes) as separate nodes. In contrast, OmniDeps and Depends represent C and
C++ at the type level, extracting structs, classes, and unions, and grouping
functions under their enclosing types.

Because Arcan outputs file paths while OmniDeps outputs type names, their
node sets do not intersect ($V_{\text{Arcan}} \cap V_{\text{OmniDeps}} =
\emptyset$). For this reason, direct pairwise comparison yields near-zero
Jaccard overlap in Table \ref{tab:results_arcan} ($J_{\text{nodes}} \le 0.012$,
$J_{\text{edges}} \le 0.004$).
This difference illustrates how tools designed for smell detection often
prioritize file-level organization over type hierarchies when analyzing
procedural code.

generate synthetic intermediate package nodes
when explicit package declarations are absent.

\paragraph{Subtyping and Interface Implementation (\texttt{isChildOf}
and \texttt{isImplementationOf}).}
For inheritance and interface implementation, OmniDeps captures most
of the hierarchy edges extracted by Depends
(omission rate $\Delta_{B \setminus A} = 8.7\%$) and Arcan
($\Delta_{B \setminus A} = 2.4\%$).
The discrepancy observed for \texttt{isImplementationOf} stems from
taxonomy mapping differences:
Arcan separates class extension (\texttt{extend}, mapped to
\texttt{isChildOf}) from interface implementation
(\texttt{implement}, mapped to \texttt{isImplementationOf}). In
contrast, both OmniDeps and Depends group class
inheritance and interface implementation under a unified subtyping
relation (\texttt{isChildOf}).
As a result, OmniDeps does not emit edges labeled
\texttt{isImplementationOf}, but captures interface contracts under
\texttt{isChildOf}.

\paragraph{Behavioral Coupling and Method Calls (\texttt{dependsOn}).}
For behavioral and call-graph dependencies, OmniDeps and Depends
share an average of 27,891 edges per project in Java.
The discrepancy rates between the two tools are balanced: $\Delta_{A
\setminus B} = 26.1\%$ and $\Delta_{B \setminus A} = 26.1\%$.
OmniDeps extracts field-level type references and constructor
instantiations that Depends omits, while Depends
(which incorporates compiled classpath dependencies and standard library symbol tables)
resolves chained method calls traversing standard library or external types,
where OmniDeps deliberately bounds extraction to source code without loading
external binaries.

\paragraph{Import Directives (\texttt{includes}).}
In Java, Arcan removes file-level \texttt{import} statements entirely
($\Delta_{B \setminus A} = 0.0\%$), retaining only
resolved type references. Depends retains imports at the file level;
when these are lifted to their enclosing classes
during graph harmonization, they appear as package- or class-level inclusions.

\paragraph{Header Inlining and Type Visibility in C and C++.}
\label{subsubsec:header_inlining}
In C and C++, differences between extractors are strongly influenced
by header inclusion.
When translation units are preprocessed (\texttt{clang -E}), headers
are expanded directly into each source file.
Any type defined in an included header (such as a shared struct) is
parsed in every translation unit where it appears,
increasing the number of duplicate nodes extracted by OmniDeps.

To test whether this duplication could be eliminated, we evaluated
stripping inlined header lines based on preprocessor
linemarkers (\texttt{\# 1 "header.h"}). However, compiler-based
static analysis tools (such as Eclipse CDT in Depends)
require complete type definitions within each compilation unit to
perform symbol resolution. Stripping header contents
led to incomplete type syntax errors in Depends, causing analysis
failures and reducing graph overlap to zero on projects
such as \texttt{curl} and \texttt{nginx}. As a result, in single-pass
analysis, translation-unit preprocessing involves
an inherent trade-off between complete type resolution and duplicate
node declarations across translation units.

\subsection{RQ4: Runtime Scalability, Throughput, and Speedup}

throughput scaling, and speedup factors of OmniDeps against Arcan,
Depends, and Entangle across the 156-project dataset.Table
\ref{tab:runtime_scalability_summary} presents the runtime and
throughput metrics broken down by programming language and comparator tool.

To ensure methodologically sound comparisons despite differing completion
rates and baseline failure modes (as detailed in Section
\ref{subsec:results_completion}), the evaluation follows a strictly
pairwise protocol:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Projects ($N$):} The mutual intersection of repositories
    successfully analyzed by both OmniDeps (Tool A) and the comparator (Tool
    B). OmniDeps' metrics vary across blocks because each tool
    successfully completed a different subset of projects.
  \item \textbf{Speedup Mean (Med):} Computed per project as $S_p = t_{B, p} /
    t_{A, p}$. We report the arithmetic mean alongside the sample median in
    parentheses, which provides a robust central measure against right-skewed
    runtime ratios.
  \item \textbf{Time and Throughput:} \textbf{Time (s)} is the average
    wall-clock execution time per project, and \textbf{Throughput} is the
    average rate in source lines of code per second ($\text{LOC} /
    \text{second}$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Analysis Throughput.}
Across the entire dataset, OmniDeps sustains an overall mean
throughput of \textbf{74,445 lines of code per second} (median 59,466
LOC/s). Throughput is highest on C (97,382 LOC/s), Rust (97,487 LOC/s),
and Java (86,601 LOC/s), analyzing most codebases in under three
seconds. In comparison, baseline tools achieve noticeably lower
throughput: Depends averages 10,270 LOC/s, Arcan averages 3,361 LOC/s,
and Entangle reaches 51,810 LOC/s on Java.

\paragraph{Speedup Relative to Baseline Analyzers.}
Runtime speedup varies depending on the baseline tool and the target language:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Against \textbf{Depends} ($N = 116$ paired projects), OmniDeps
    achieves an average speedup of \textbf{13.21x} (median 7.72x). The
    speedup is most pronounced on Python (mean 25.02x, peaking at
    74.44x on \texttt{peewee}) and on C (mean 15.25x, peaking at 79.55x
    on \texttt{sds}). On compact repositories such as \texttt{pydantic},
    Depends completed in comparable time, yielding a minimum speedup of
    0.88x.
  \item Against \textbf{Arcan} ($N = 61$ paired projects), OmniDeps
    achieves an average speedup of \textbf{48.21x} (median 29.93x),
    with individual speedups reaching up to 116.73x on Java
    (\texttt{okhttp}) and 536.60x on C (\texttt{sds}).
  \item Against \textbf{Entangle} ($N = 19$ paired Java projects),
    OmniDeps is on average \textbf{4.01x faster} (median 2.86x),
    peaking at 15.31x on \texttt{flink}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Runtime Scaling Trends.}

\ref{fig:runtime_scalability_c_cpp} plot execution runtimes against

near-linear scaling with respect to the number of lines of code. Even on
repositories exceeding one million lines of code (such as \texttt{FFmpeg},
\texttt{qemu}, \texttt{elasticsearch}, or \texttt{godot}), execution
completes within a few seconds to low tens of seconds, without
exhibiting super-linear latency spikes.

In contrast, the comparator tools show steeper scaling curves and higher
dispersion as codebase size increases. In Java and Python, Depends scales
consistently on small utilities but exhibits substantial runtime growth
on large codebases (such as \texttt{django} or \texttt{sympy}), climbing
into hundreds of seconds. Arcan exhibits both a noticeable initial
latency even on modest projects and a steeper growth curve on larger
systems, reflecting the overhead of full compiler frontend loading and
resolution. As a result, the speedup of OmniDeps tends to grow with the
scale of the target repository, making its lightweight parsing approach
particularly advantageous on large codebases.

Overall, the empirical evaluation demonstrates that OmniDeps matches or
surpasses the execution speed of existing baseline tools across all evaluated
languages. Execution times remain reliably on the order of seconds across the
vast majority of repositories, scaling predictably without runtime explosions
even on systems with hundreds of thousands or millions of lines of code. These
results confirm that the tool achieves practical efficiency and scalability
suitable for real-world software analysis pipelines.

\section{Summary of Answers to Research Questions}
\label{sec:answers_to_rqs}

This section summarizes the experimental findings by providing
direct answers to the four research questions guiding this evaluation:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.0em]
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Language Construct Coverage):}
    \textit{OmniDeps successfully captures the foundational
      structural constructs and dependencies across all five
    supported languages in controlled benchmarks.}
    The synthetic test suite confirms that Concrete Syntax Tree
    queries correctly identify modular containers, structured
    types, inheritance links, and method calls in isolated test
    cases, providing a verified baseline for real-world analysis.

  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Cross-Tool Structural Alignment):}
    \textit{OmniDeps demonstrates high structural alignment with
      established analyzers in structured object-oriented codebases,
      with lower edge overlap in dynamically typed languages and
    procedural code.}
    In Java, OmniDeps aligns closely with all three comparators
    ($J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.814$, $J_{\text{edges}} = 0.587$, and
    only 2.1\% entity omission against Depends). In Python, entity
    alignment achieves $J_{\text{nodes}} = 0.852$ (median $0.882$) with an entity
    omission rate of just $4.8\%$, while edge agreement reaches $0.323$ overall
    and $0.368$ (median $0.387$) when isolating behavioral interactions. In C and C++,
    translation-unit preprocessing substantially improves edge
    agreement with Depends (from $0.081$ to $0.165$ in C++).

  \item \textbf{RQ3 (Discrepancy Analysis Across Dependency Types):}
    \textit{Discrepancies concentrate in behavioral call-graph
      resolution and in differences of representation granularity,
    while containment hierarchies show near-complete agreement.}
    On Java, containment hierarchies exhibit low omission rates
    ($\le 1.8\%$), whereas behavioral call edges reflect the
    trade-off between source-level syntax extraction and compiler-backed
    resolution of standard library and external classpath types.
    Furthermore, tools designed for smell
    detection (like Arcan in C/C++) model code at the file level,
    producing disjoint vertex sets compared to type-level graphs.

  \item \textbf{RQ4 (Computational Scalability and Throughput):}
    \textit{OmniDeps achieves substantial speedups over existing
      tools, scaling essentially linearly with codebase size and
      completing execution in the order of seconds across the vast
    majority of repositories.}
    OmniDeps sustains an average throughput of 74,445 LOC/s,
    yielding average speedups of 13.2x over Depends, 48.2x over
    Arcan, and 4.0x over Entangle, while avoiding runtime
    explosions even on codebases exceeding one million lines of code.
\end{itemize}
